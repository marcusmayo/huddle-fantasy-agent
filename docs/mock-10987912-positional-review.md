# Forward Progress 10987912: positional-mix retrospective

Reviewed September 7–8, 2026, immediately after the live mock. This evaluates roster construction separately from its verified 15 manual selections and complete reconciliation.

## Judgment

Six running backs was legal and can be a defensible strategy in this format. It is not, by itself, proof of a bad draft. However, I would not endorse this **6 RB / 4 WR / 2 QB** build as Huddle's best default for an eight-team league. Four bench RBs and a backup QB left only one bench WR behind the two WR starters and the WR occupying Flex. The sixth RB and second QB need a stronger marginal-value justification than Huddle currently provides.

My preferred starting framework with an established QB and TE is **1 QB / 5 RB / 6 WR / 1 TE / 1 K / 1 DEF**. A reasonable insurance variant is **2 QB / 5 RB / 5 WR / 1 TE / 1 K / 1 DEF**. These are my review recommendations for this particular 15-player, six-bench mock, not universal rules or experimentally proven optima. An unusually valuable sixth RB can still justify departing from them. No outcome or additional fantasy-point gain can be guaranteed.

## What Huddle actually considers

The production balanced score includes value over replacement (28%), scarcity (16%), positional need (17%), next-turn urgency (14%), upside (7%), floor (6%), and consensus (12%), with risk and phase penalties. These are heuristic weights on normalized components, not percentages of expected fantasy points. It also checks position maximums and ensures enough picks remain to fill starters.

The audit found several limitations:

1. **Bench saturation barely changes need.** For this roster, the fractional RB and WR target is 2⅓. At three drafted players, raw need is 0.14; at four, five, or six it is the same 0.04. This does not create a progressively stronger cost for RB5 and RB6.
2. **Soft need does not use the actual Flex assignment.** The target divides one Flex equally across RB, WR, and TE. With one TE, it can retain a fractional TE need even after RB/WR already cover every relevant starter slot. The hard legality/coverage matcher handles Flex correctly; the scoring need term is a separate, cruder calculation.
3. **Replacement is not modeled from projected waivers or our own depth.** The baseline takes roughly starter demand times team count from the currently available positional list. Late in the draft, that can value a player against a very deep remaining option without accounting for already-filled league demand, the existing roster, or a limited 100-row observation window.
4. **There is no expected-lineup contribution calculation.** The engine does not estimate bench players' likely starts, injury/bye coverage, improvement over existing Flex options, or the points obtainable from a waiver replacement. A legal complete roster is a weaker objective than maximum expected scored points.
5. **Mock upside is an estimate, not independent breakout evidence.** Floor and ceiling are generated from the same Yahoo season projection using a fixed spread. They do not establish contingent workload, breakout probability, or real weekly volatility. Exact player-ID crosswalks may contribute provider evidence, but unmatched names do not inherit it.
6. **The mock snapshot omits Yahoo's position-limit values.** The scorer supports configured maxima, but its RB fallback for this roster is nine (three eligible starting slots plus six bench). The completed roster happened to obey Yahoo's actual six-RB limit. Future mock snapshots should explicitly carry and verify the observed limits.

## Recommended scoring improvement

Use **expected additional points in legal weekly lineups** as the primary value being estimated. For each candidate, compare the best feasible lineup with and without the player across supported weekly projections, byes, and transparent injury/role scenarios. Credit useful coverage and breakout potential while subtracting the value of realistic waiver alternatives. Report uncertainty when the required data is missing; do not present a generic percentage spread as researched upside.

Then add soft composition controls derived from league size, starter demand, existing strength, Flex usage, bench size, and position limits. For this roster, adding RB5/RB6 or a second QB should trigger an explicit comparison with WR depth. A clearly superior opportunity should be able to override the soft target. Only actual Yahoo rules should be hard caps.

A practical recommendation should explain: current mix, likely starter/Flex/bench role, remaining needs, best alternative at another position, and the reason the preferred player contributes more. Next-turn scarcity should help decide when to take a useful player, not substitute for evidence that the player helps the roster.

Before deploying a new scoring model, validate that it prefers useful WR coverage over a comparable sixth RB, still accepts an exceptional RB bargain, avoids artificial TE need after Flex is covered, adjusts to eight versus twelve teams and two versus three WR starters, obeys actual position limits, and preserves draft speed. Save every pre-pick snapshot and recommendation breakdown for auditable replays. Do not tune the model merely to force this one draft into a predetermined count.

This review does not claim that scoring changes have already been implemented or that a replacement draft would score a quantified number of extra points. The deployed throughput, identity, and Flex-capacity fixes passed the live execution test; positional value optimization is the next distinct change to validate.
